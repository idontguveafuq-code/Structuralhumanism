# Gods of Empire

## Inherited Scripture, Borrowed Theology, and the Politics of Sacred Authority

KP | Journal of Comparative Religion and Historical Theology | 2025

> **ABSTRACT**
>
> This paper examines the historical and archaeological evidence that the theological world inherited by the Abrahamic traditions emerged through cultural borrowing, textual development, and political consolidation rather than through a simple unbroken transmission of fixed doctrine. It considers Mesopotamian narrative parallels to Genesis, Persian and Zoroastrian influence on post-exilic Jewish theology, archaeological evidence for Yahwistic polytheism within ancient Israel, and the explicitly political conditions under which the King James Bible was produced. Its argument is not that spiritual life is fraudulent, but that institutional religious authority has often depended on presenting historically layered traditions as untouched revelation. The relevant target is therefore not faith itself, but the recurrent use of sacred language to stabilize concentrated power. [file:14]
>
> **Keywords:** biblical origins, Zoroastrian influence, Kuntillet Ajrud, Asherah, Israelite religion, King James Bible, political theology, sacred authority

## I. The History Beneath Inheritance

The paper begins from a simple but destabilizing claim: most believers inherit a religious story about origins that is much cleaner than the historical record permits. They are taught continuity where the evidence often shows adaptation, borrowing, conflict, reform, and selective preservation. That gap between devotional narrative and scholarly history is the entry point of the essay, and it is what gives the piece both its force and its risk. [file:14]

Handled carefully, the point is not that faith communities are uniquely deceptive. It is that institutions built around sacred authority have strong incentives to simplify their own formation story. Traditions gain stability when their internal development is hidden, when theological layers are flattened into timeless certainty, and when historical contingency is treated as threat rather than context. [file:14]

The most effective version of the argument therefore targets function rather than identity. The recurring actor is not one religion or one people, but institutional power using claims of sacred continuity to insulate itself from critical scrutiny. That framing is essential, because without it the essay would be easier to caricature as hostility to belief rather than a historical critique of authority. [file:14]

## II. The Stories Came First Elsewhere

The Mesopotamian material is among the paper's strongest sections because it deals in narrative structures too specific to dismiss lightly. The flood account in Genesis closely parallels much older Mesopotamian traditions, especially the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, not only in broad theme but in details such as divine warning, construction of a vessel, release of birds, mountain landing, and post-flood sacrifice. At a minimum, this places the Genesis story within a wider ancient Near Eastern narrative world rather than outside history as a wholly isolated revelation. [file:14]

That point matters for more than literary comparison. Once readers grasp that major biblical narratives belong to a preexisting cultural environment, the entire theological imagination of origins becomes less insulated from history. The text no longer appears as if it descended into the world without neighbors. It appears as a product of people living among other peoples, inheriting stories, reshaping them, and giving them new covenantal meaning. [file:14]

The Abraham material reinforces this. If the founding patriarch of the tradition is himself located in Mesopotamia by the biblical text, then the broader claim of deep Near Eastern continuity becomes harder to resist. The issue is not whether the biblical tradition says something distinctive. It is whether distinctiveness is the same thing as historical isolation. The paper rightly insists that it is not. [file:14]

## III. Persian Afterlives

The section on Zoroastrian influence is conceptually crucial because it addresses elements many believers assume have always belonged to biblical religion in their familiar form. The paper argues that the more developed figure of Satan, a moralized afterlife, and apocalyptic dualism become more legible in Jewish tradition after sustained contact with Persian religious culture during and after the exile. This does not mean Judaism or Christianity simply copied Zoroastrianism. It means that ideas often presented as timeless theological givens may have emerged through historical exchange. [file:14]

The example of “Paradise” is especially effective because it gives readers something concrete. A Persian loan word preserved inside the theological vocabulary of reward is a small linguistic fossil of larger cultural contact. It condenses a broad historical argument into a single memorable sign that traditions absorb more from neighboring worlds than later orthodoxy usually admits. [file:14]

This part of the essay works best when it avoids overstating precision. Cultural transmission in antiquity is complex, multi-directional, and often impossible to reduce to one linear path. But the paper does not need maximal certainty to make its case. It is enough to show that post-exilic theological developments bear meaningful structural resemblance to Persian religious concepts and that this resemblance emerges under conditions of actual historical contact. ## IV. The God of Israel Was Not Alone

The archaeological section is the paper's most concrete and potentially most disruptive. The Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions provide material evidence that some ancient Israelites associated YHWH with Asherah, a goddess known from the wider Canaanite religious world. Whatever debates persist over the exact interpretation of “his Asherah,” the existence of these inscriptions at all is enough to unsettle the popular image of pristine original monotheism. [file:14]

This evidence matters because it comes from the ground rather than from later polemic. It corroborates what the Hebrew Bible itself often suggests indirectly: that prophetic monotheism emerged through struggle against widespread popular religious plurality rather than describing an uncontested national baseline. The prophets were not condemning hypothetical temptations. They were confronting living practices embedded in the religious life of ancient Israel and Judah. [file:14]

The section on Josiah's reforms sharpens this point further. If the biblical text itself records Asherah-related objects, cultic practices, and other non-exclusive worship even in relation to the Jerusalem temple, then the difference between popular religion and later canonical memory becomes impossible to ignore. The historical story is not one of occasional deviation from a stable monotheism. It is a long contest over what counted as legitimate worship. [file:14]

## V. Translation in the Service of Rule

The King James Bible section broadens the essay from ancient development to early modern political theology. Its core claim is not that the translation lacks literary greatness or spiritual significance. It is that the production of scripture in English was inseparable from the political needs of crown, church, and social order. Translation choices are never neutral, and in this case the evidence of royal instruction makes the institutional purpose unusually visible. [file:14]

The paper is especially effective when it points to specific translation directives such as preserving “church” rather than “congregation” and maintaining terms that reinforced episcopal hierarchy. These are not abstract suspicions about bias. They are examples of power attempting to stabilize itself through the language by which ordinary believers would encounter divine authority. The choice of “Lord” as a habitual rendering that harmonized divine sovereignty with terrestrial hierarchy deepens the same concern. [file:14]

This is one of the essay's most important insights: political uses of scripture do not always begin with censorship or open coercion. They often begin in the quieter work of translation, framing, authorized wording, and institutional control over which version of the sacred is permitted to sound natural. [file:14]

## VI. Faith and Authority Are Not the Same

The essay's most necessary distinction arrives near the end. To historicize a tradition is not the same thing as to erase its spiritual value. Communities can still find truth, beauty, moral wisdom, and sacred depth in texts that emerged through layered historical processes. What the historical record threatens is not faith itself, but the claim of institutional authorities to possess unquestionable legitimacy on the basis of an origin story too simplified to withstand scholarship. [file:14]

That distinction is what allows the argument to remain serious rather than merely provocative. The point is not to humiliate believers with information they were never given. The point is to ask what kind of religious life becomes possible once faith is separated from the political uses of certainty. A tradition that survives honest history may ultimately be more durable than one defended by suppression, selective teaching, and inherited illusion. [file:14]

This is also where the essay aligns most strongly with the wider manuscript's recurring language about concentrated power. Sacred authority becomes politically dangerous when it is made opaque, insulated from criticism, and fused with civic obedience. Once those functions are named, the problem becomes institutional and historical rather than tribal. [file:14]

## VII. Conclusion

The revised title, *Gods of Empire*, captures the paper's strongest suggestion: religious inheritance is never only theological. It is also imperial, linguistic, political, and institutional. Stories are transmitted, doctrines are reworked, gods are elevated or suppressed, and translations are authorized under conditions shaped by power as much as by piety. [file:14]

The essay does not need to prove that every believer has been lied to in the same way to make its case. It needs only to show that the traditions many people receive as untouched revelation were formed through contest, borrowing, reform, and governance. On that point, the paper is persuasive. [file:14]

Its deepest challenge is therefore not against belief but against passivity. If sacred texts, doctrines, and religious institutions have histories, then those histories belong to the people governed by them. Recovering that history is not desecration. It is part of what intellectual and spiritual adulthood require. [file:14]
