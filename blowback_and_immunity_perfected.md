# Blowback and Immunity

## How Unaccountable Power Creates Enemies and Escapes Consequence

KP | Journal of International Security and Political History | 2025

> **ABSTRACT**
>
> This paper examines several foreign-policy cases in which covert action, strategic manipulation, or suppressed accountability produced long-term consequences while insulating decision-makers from meaningful public reckoning. It traces the role of U.S. support for Afghan militant networks in the conditions from which al-Qaeda emerged, the role of post-invasion policy in Iraq in creating the organizational space for ISIS, the documented Israeli policy of facilitating Qatari transfers to Hamas as part of a divide-and-manage strategy, the unresolved attack on the USS *Liberty*, and the Kennedy-era effort to require the American Zionist Council to register under the Foreign Agents Registration Act. The argument is not that one actor explains every case. It is that the same institutional pattern recurs: concentrated power acting through secrecy, strategic calculation, and selective law enforcement while the public absorbs the consequences. [file:15]
>
> **Keywords:** blowback, covert action, al-Qaeda, ISIS, Hamas, USS Liberty, FARA, foreign lobbying, accountability, democratic oversight

## I. The Question Iraq Left Behind

The paper begins with an essential democratic question: if the public was misled into a catastrophic war in Iraq and no one faced equivalent legal consequence, what else has been done under similar conditions of impunity? That is the correct entry point, because Iraq was not merely a policy error. It was an institutional event in which false or baseless claims justified mass violence, enormous expenditure, and lasting regional damage without the kind of accountability that a functioning democracy would treat as mandatory. [file:15]

Once that precedent is understood, skepticism ceases to be paranoia and becomes civic responsibility. The burden shifts. Citizens no longer need to assume institutional honesty until disproven in each case; they are entitled to examine the record of covert policy, strategic deception, and selective enforcement with greater rigor. This paper offers several case studies for exactly that reason. [file:15]

The unifying frame is important. The subject is not a people, religion, or nationality. It is a pattern of behavior: concentrated institutional power pursuing strategic goals through secrecy, using public ignorance as a resource, and escaping consequence when those strategies fail or produce catastrophe. [file:15]

## II. Afghanistan and the Logic of Blowback

The discussion of Operation Cyclone is strongest when treated not as a morality play but as a case of imperial short-termism. The CIA's support for Afghan mujahideen networks, expanded dramatically during the anti-Soviet campaign, was justified in immediate strategic terms. Yet the same networks, infrastructures, and militant ecosystems later shaped the environment from which al-Qaeda emerged. When Brzezinski later dismissed the long-term cost in comparison with Soviet defeat, he articulated a mindset more revealing than any single document: tactical geopolitical advantage was treated as worth the downstream radicalization. [file:15]

This is the logic of blowback in its clearest form. The relevant point is not that every later extremist formation can be reduced mechanically to one covert program. It is that covert action often creates durable armed environments whose future uses are not controlled by their original sponsors. Once money, weapons, training, and transnational militant logistics enter a conflict zone at scale, they do not remain tidy instruments of policy. They become a legacy. [file:15]

The democratic failure lies not only in the original decision but in the later public narrative. Institutions that helped create the conditions for a threat often reappear as the institutions demanding expanded secrecy and military power to confront that same threat. The public is asked to fund the solution without being permitted to fully examine the role prior policy played in producing the problem. [file:15]

## III. Iraq and the Making of ISIS

The section on ISIS deepens the argument by showing how destruction itself can become infrastructure. The dissolution of the Iraqi army and security services after the 2003 invasion was not merely a bureaucratic error. It was a decision that instantly produced a large pool of armed, trained, humiliated, and unemployed men with organizational experience and reasons to resist the occupation order. The subsequent emergence of ISIS drew heavily on exactly that reservoir of military and administrative capacity. [file:15]

This matters because it exposes the distance between official war narratives and actual strategic consequence. The invasion was sold in part through claims linking Iraq to threats the U.S. government's own later inquiries did not substantiate. Yet the invasion helped create conditions for a genuinely more dangerous militant landscape than the one it claimed to preempt. In that sense, the war did not simply fail to solve the problem it named. It participated in manufacturing a successor problem. [file:15]

The phrase “manufactured enemies” is especially apt here, provided it is understood structurally. The claim is not that states literally design every adversary from scratch. The claim is that policy choices made by powerful states often generate the conditions under which later enemies become more likely, more coherent, and more militarily capable. That is enough to require a much higher standard of public accountability than these decisions have typically received. [file:15]

## IV. Divide and Manage

The Hamas section extends the argument from blowback to strategic maintenance of fragmentation. The core claim is that Netanyahu's government permitted or facilitated Qatari transfers to Hamas as part of a policy designed to keep Palestinian political leadership divided, thereby weakening the prospects of unified diplomacy and a viable two-state framework. What makes this section significant is that the documentation comes largely from Israeli journalists, officials, and commentators rather than from outside ideological antagonists. [file:15]

The broader lesson is not confined to Israel-Palestine. States frequently prefer manageable fragmentation to coherent opposition. An adversary split into rival camps can be monitored, manipulated, and politically discredited more easily than one capable of unified negotiation. Yet the strategy carries obvious danger. Actors treated as useful instruments in one phase of policy can become sources of mass violence in another, at which point the same public that was never consulted about the strategy is asked to absorb its consequences. [file:15]

This pattern closely resembles the logic seen in other theaters. Strategic actors elevate short-term control over long-term stability, then describe the resulting catastrophe as though it emerged ex nihilo from irrational hatred. The effect is to erase prior policy choices from public memory and to preserve the immunity of those who made them. [file:15]

## V. The USS *Liberty* and the Refusal of Reckoning

The *Liberty* section is about more than one attack. It is about what it means when thirty-four American servicemen die, the official investigation is later described by its own senior legal counsel as predetermined, and Congress never conducts the kind of searching inquiry the event would ordinarily demand. Whatever one's final conclusion about intent, the record described here raises a democratic issue of the first order: whether some incidents become effectively uninvestigable because the political costs of truth are judged too high. [file:15]

That is what makes the Ward Boston affidavit so important within the paper's architecture. It shifts the focus from disputed interpretation of battlefield facts to the integrity of the accountability process itself. A democracy does not have to resolve every historical controversy instantly, but it cannot remain healthy if major events involving the deaths of its own personnel are insulated from serious investigation by prior political decision. [file:15]

In this sense, the *Liberty* case belongs in the essay not only as a foreign-policy controversy, but as a study in selective visibility. Some deaths are national catechisms. Others are administratively closed. The paper argues that this asymmetry is not morally neutral. It reflects the operation of political priorities that can override the ordinary expectation of truth-telling. [file:15]

## VI. Law for Some, Exemption for Others

The FARA section provides the clearest example of selective enforcement as a democratic problem. The Kennedy administration's effort to require the American Zionist Council to register under the Foreign Agents Registration Act demonstrates that the relevant legal question was once treated as serious within the U.S. government itself. The fact that the pressure receded after Kennedy's assassination, and that successor lobbying structures continued without equivalent registration, is important less as a single dramatic revelation than as evidence of uneven state willingness to apply its own transparency rules. [file:15]

This point is especially important to handle carefully. The issue is not ethnicity or religion. It is the integrity of law. A democracy cannot claim legitimacy if statutes designed to inform the public about foreign influence are applied aggressively to some cases and effectively suspended in others where the political costs of enforcement are greater. Uniform application of law is not a secondary procedural ideal. It is one of the few things preventing foreign-policy formation from becoming wholly opaque to the citizens expected to live with its results. [file:15]

By placing this section after the others, the paper reveals an important continuity. Covert action, strategic manipulation, suppressed investigation, and selective registration all depend on the same enabling condition: a public denied equal access to the truth about who is acting, on whose behalf, and with what consequences. [file:15]

## VII. Pattern, Not People

This essay's strongest analytical safeguard is its insistence that the recurring actor is a type of institutional behavior, not a tribe. That formulation has to remain central. The moment the analysis drifts toward population blame, it loses both its moral seriousness and its explanatory precision. The cases assembled here do not require civilizational caricature to be disturbing. They are disturbing on their own terms. [file:15]

What connects them is not a hidden metaphysical enemy but a familiar civic pathology: concentrated power operating beyond ordinary scrutiny, producing disasters the public is later asked to finance, mourn, or fear without being fully allowed to understand. That pattern can appear in Washington, in allied governments, in intelligence services, in lobbying systems, and in media environments that decline to pursue the implications of their own reporting. [file:15]

To keep the argument at its best, the target must remain specific: secrecy, unaccountable strategy, selective enforcement, and immunity from consequence. Those are the mechanisms. Once named clearly, they are more than enough. [file:15]

## VIII. Conclusion

The phrase “manufactured enemies” captures a real and unsettling truth, but it needs precision. The most defensible version of the claim is not that powerful institutions invent every threat from nothing. It is that they repeatedly help create, intensify, arm, divide, misread, or politically exploit the conditions from which later threats emerge. They then ask the public to confront those threats without fully reckoning with the policies that made them more likely. [file:15]

That cycle is corrosive to democratic life because it combines strategic recklessness with narrative control. The same institutions that act without transparency often retain disproportionate power to define what the public remembers about the aftermath. Blowback is rebranded as surprise. Selective enforcement is normalized as pragmatism. Impunity is hidden inside statecraft. [file:15]

A serious democracy would treat these cases not as isolated scandals but as warnings about structure. It would ask what mechanisms of oversight, transparency, declassification, and legal consistency are necessary to prevent strategic power from repeatedly outrunning public consent. Without those reforms, foreign policy remains one of the clearest places where concentrated power can create danger, survive the consequences, and return asking for more authority than before. [file:15]
