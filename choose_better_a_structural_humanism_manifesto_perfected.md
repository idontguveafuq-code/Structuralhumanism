# Choose Better

## A Structural Humanism Manifesto

Kai Price | 2025

> **ABSTRACT**
>
> *Choose Better* argues that many of the crises shaping contemporary American life are not isolated failures but connected expressions of the same underlying pattern: concentrated power insulated from democratic accountability. Across media, debt, healthcare, war, religion, surveillance, and public finance, the book tracks how institutions convert dependence into compliance while persuading the public to experience those arrangements as normal, inevitable, or even freely chosen. Its organizing claim is not that history is driven by a single villain or a single tribe, but that the same institutional behaviors recur across eras in different forms. The book's answer is not despair or indiscriminate rage. It is structural clarity, moral seriousness, democratic accountability, and a disciplined refusal to mistake managed helplessness for freedom. ## Preface

What is government for? Before policy, before ideology, before party, that is the first question. If the answer is the protection of life, liberty, dignity, and the individual against arbitrary power, then many of the systems governing contemporary American life must be judged not by their rhetoric but by their effects. By that standard, this book argues, too many of them have turned against the people they claim to serve. [file:1]

The pages that follow are not built on the assumption that every institution is corrupt in every respect. They are built on a narrower and more defensible claim: when power becomes concentrated, financially rewarded, and weakly supervised, it predictably begins to shape rules in its own interest. The names of the actors change across domains. The mechanisms do not. The result is a public repeatedly disciplined by debt, managed by information systems, narrowed by inherited dogmas, and distracted from the structural arrangements producing its pain. [file:1]

The argument of this book is not that ordinary people are foolish. It is that they are managed. It is not that disagreement is unreal. It is that disagreement is often intensified, redirected, and harvested by systems that benefit from keeping people divided while decisions of lasting consequence occur elsewhere. To recognize this is not to become cynical. It is to begin seeing politically. [file:1]

The title *Choose Better* is therefore not a slogan of self-help or a vague moral aspiration. It is a political and civic demand. A country can choose better than a healthcare system that extracts from the sick, a debt system that turns dependence into discipline, a media system that narrows what is sayable, a foreign policy insulated from public consequence, and a spiritual inheritance too often confused with the political interests that shaped it. The choice is difficult. It is not impossible. [file:1]

## Part One: The Systems That Own Us

Part One examines four interlocking mechanisms: managed tribalism, debt as social control, information systems that narrow public awareness, and the political uses of sacred language. These chapters are not offered as isolated complaints. They are the book's foundation, because they describe the recurring methods through which concentrated power prevents broad democratic solidarity from becoming effective. [file:1]

### Chapter One: Manufactured Division

The first mechanism is division. Every durable hierarchy must solve the same basic problem: what happens if the people living under it begin to recognize their shared interests more clearly than the identities that separate them? The solution, historically, has been to intensify those identities and to organize conflict around them. This does not mean all differences are fake or all conflict is manufactured. It means that the level, texture, and persistence of hostility in modern political life often exceed what ordinary disagreement would produce on its own. [file:1]

The manuscript's image of September 12, 2001, functions as a counterexample to the claim that contemporary fragmentation is simply human nature. In moments when spectacle, emergency, or catastrophe temporarily interrupts the management of attention, people often rediscover solidarities wider than tribe. The argument here is that this wider solidarity is not an illusion produced by crisis. It may be closer to the baseline capacity from which the ordinary political environment repeatedly pulls us away. [file:1]

The chapter grounds this claim through both historical and contemporary examples. COINTELPRO provides a documented case of state-sponsored fragmentation aimed at preventing coalition across racial and class lines. The social media era supplies a privatized update: platform incentives that reward outrage, fear, and tribal performance because those emotions hold attention and attention produces profit. In both cases the core pattern is the same. Division is not merely suffered. It is cultivated where it serves institutional interests. [file:1]

The practical lesson is simple and severe. When public hostility becomes intense, the first question should not always be which side is morally pure. It should often be who benefits from the fight. That question does not erase genuine differences. It disciplines them. It turns anger away from broad human targets and back toward structures that profit from keeping the public exhausted, suspicious, and politically disorganized. [file:1]

### Chapter Two: Debt as Governance

The second mechanism is debt. This chapter is among the manuscript's strongest because it frames debt not merely as finance but as a relationship of power. Debt compels behavior not through overt command but through the quiet narrowing of options. A person burdened by medical bills, student loans, payday obligations, or insecure credit is not simply poorer. That person is easier to discipline, slower to resist, and less able to imagine risk on democratic terms. [file:1]

The book's historical argument, drawing on examples from ancient debt jubilees, medieval Europe, colonial India, Haiti, and modern consumer finance, is that debt becomes politically decisive when it is abstracted from reciprocal human obligation and made transferable, interest-bearing, and enforceable by institutions indifferent to the life of the debtor. The comparison across eras is not meant to flatten moral differences between those cases. It is meant to reveal a recurring mechanism: convert dependence into paperwork, formalize the extraction, and then call the resulting subordination voluntary. [file:1]

The contemporary American examples make the point concrete. Medical debt is often incurred without meaningful price transparency or bargaining power. Student debt is signed under intense cultural pressure by young people asked to make life-altering decisions under deep information asymmetry. The larger claim is not that every borrower is coerced in the same way. It is that the legal and economic architecture around debt is designed to produce a more compliant public by ensuring that millions of people cannot afford interruption, experimentation, or organized dissent. [file:1]

This chapter's most resonant formulation is that debt is the quietest leash ever devised. It does not need to jail people to constrain them. It only needs to keep them one missed payment away from panic. The effect is political as much as financial. A population structurally burdened in this way is less available for the work of self-government. [file:1]

### Chapter Three: The Permitted Narrative

The third mechanism is information control. The chapter's emphasis is not on crude censorship but on narrowing: the managed range of what becomes visible, discussable, and institutionally legible in mainstream public life. Media consolidation, advertiser dependence, elite sourcing, and algorithmic amplification do not require a single conspiratorial editor deciding what the country may think. They work through structure. [file:1]

The book draws on the consolidation of American media ownership, the Sinclair must-run episode, the Facebook Files, and the growth of warrantless surveillance powers to show how information systems can simultaneously shape what the public sees and what the state can know about the public. The effect is not omnipotence. It is asymmetry. Citizens encounter a curated field of visibility while the institutions governing them accumulate tools of observation, prediction, and response. [file:1]

The most important insight here is that the censorship most people should fear is not only direct prohibition. It is the quiet absence of ideas, facts, and frameworks that never become familiar enough to enter ordinary political vocabulary. Once this narrowing is recognized, public ignorance appears less like a failure of intelligence and more like a predictable outcome of infrastructural design. [file:1]

### Chapter Four: Sacred or Convenient

The fourth mechanism concerns religion, inherited meaning, and the political uses of the sacred. This is the manuscript's most delicate chapter and one of its most original. Its claim is not that faith is inherently deceptive or that spiritual life should be abandoned. Its claim is that the institutional forms through which revelation, scripture, and doctrine are transmitted have histories shaped by editors, councils, monarchs, empires, and political needs. [file:1]

Archaeological evidence, textual history, Persian influence on later biblical cosmology, and politically consequential translation choices in the King James Bible are used to argue that what many believers inherit as timeless and untouched often arrived through highly contingent historical processes. The important distinction is between what is sacred and what is convenient: the recurring moral insights shared across traditions versus the institutional accretions that helped authorities stabilize rule, define orthodoxy, and manage populations. [file:1]

This chapter matters to the larger argument because power is never maintained by force alone. It also depends on legitimacy. If religious language has been used repeatedly to bless hierarchy, extraction, war, or obedience, then examining how that language was shaped is not an attack on faith. It is part of democratic literacy. The chapter's strongest move is to insist that serious faith should be able to survive honest history. [file:1]

## Part Two: The History We Were Not Taught

If Part One maps the mechanisms, Part Two turns to the historical record those mechanisms help obscure. Here the manuscript shifts from analysis of structure to a series of case studies on American power, foreign policy, covert action, surveillance, and political economy. The animating claim is not that every event has one hidden cause, but that a recognizable pattern becomes visible when official justifications are placed alongside material outcomes and declassified records. [file:1]

### Dollars, Oil, and Force

One of the book's central geopolitical arguments concerns the petrodollar system. By tracing the collapse of dollar-gold convertibility, the subsequent agreement with Saudi Arabia, and the requirement that oil-importing nations acquire dollars, the manuscript argues that American monetary privilege has been secured not only by economic trust but by political and military arrangements that the public rarely discusses in plain terms. [file:1]

The book does not reduce wars in Iraq, Libya, or pressure campaigns against Venezuela to a single motive. It is more careful than that. Instead, it argues that once the structural importance of dollar-denominated oil is recognized, certain recurring patterns become harder to dismiss as coincidence. Public justifications vary by case. The strategic beneficiary often looks similar. That argument, whether one finds every example equally persuasive or not, gives the foreign-policy chapters a strong throughline. [file:1]

### Manufactured Enemies and Managed Memory

Related chapters on 9/11, documented covert operations, and the making of long-term enemies extend the same logic. The book argues that some of the threats used to justify permanent security expansion were intensified, redirected, or exploited by prior policy choices, and that the public record of these processes has often been softened, fragmented, or pushed outside mainstream awareness. Again, the key emphasis is structural: the issue is not a single secret masterplan but an institutional environment in which war, secrecy, and public amnesia reinforce one another. [file:1]

### Extraction at Home

The foreign-policy sections are matched by domestic chapters on healthcare, education, accounting failure, and what the manuscript calls the Performance Republic. These chapters argue that the country is wealthy enough to provide dignified care, education, housing, and public goods, but that existing priorities direct massive resources elsewhere. The recurring question is not whether the United States can afford a more humane social arrangement. It is why it repeatedly chooses not to build one while funding structures that entrench dependence, opacity, and militarized capacity. [file:1]

## The Book's Method

The book's strength lies in its consistency of pattern recognition. At its best, it repeatedly reminds the reader that analysis should focus on institutional behavior rather than on ethnic, religious, or national blame. This is one of the most important editorial principles carried through the revision. The target is the pattern, not the people. Across chapters, that means concentrated power, weak accountability, managed attention, legalized extraction, and the conversion of public dependence into private leverage. [file:1]

Another strength is the insistence that moral seriousness requires specificity. The text is at its most persuasive when it names a documented mechanism, explains how it operates, and then asks what kind of public life that mechanism produces. This revised version therefore trims some of the manuscript's more repetitive intensifiers while preserving its urgency, because the evidence and the structure of the case are stronger when they do not need rhetorical overstatement. [file:1]

The voice has also been unified around several recurring formulations already present in the wider body of work: concentrated power, managed division, structural extraction, democratic accountability, and the distinction between what is sacred and what is politically convenient. Those phrases now work more consistently as anchors rather than appearing as variations that slightly compete with one another. [file:1]

## The Civic Answer

A manuscript this wide-ranging needs an answer larger than complaint. The answer offered here is not utopian and not violent. It is civic. The book calls for a public capable of seeing structural arrangements clearly enough to stop mistaking them for fate. It asks readers to reject the satisfactions of tribal hatred, to understand why debt and information systems produce passivity, to recover the democratic habit of asking who benefits from a given arrangement, and to organize around institutions rather than fantasies of redemptive vengeance. [file:1]

That answer becomes clearest in the manuscript's later emphasis on lawful pressure, public accounting, anti-corruption structures, recallability, and a civilization that could redirect existing wealth toward health, education, housing, and democratic responsiveness. The message is not that the country must become perfect before it can become better. It is that better priorities are already legible, materially possible, and morally overdue. [file:1]

## Conclusion

*Choose Better* is ultimately a book about civic adulthood. It asks whether a democratic people can learn to look past the stories that flatter their passivity and instead study the mechanisms actually shaping their lives. Its answer is demanding but not nihilistic. The chains are real, but they are not invisible once named. And once named, they can no longer be mistaken for freedom without conscious surrender. [file:1]

The final imperative of the book remains the right one. Not because every argument in it will persuade every reader equally, and not because every chapter settles every debate, but because the core moral pressure is sound: a society that knows better is obligated to choose better. The task is not someday. It is political, civic, and immediate. [file:1]
