# Consent and Oblivion

## Power, Debt, Managed Knowledge, and the Politics of Historical Erasure

Journal of Critical History and Political Economy | 2025

> **ABSTRACT**
>
> This article examines how modern systems of power reproduce themselves through more than law or overt coercion. It argues that hegemony, debt dependence, media concentration, politically useful theology, and the selective marginalization of historical evidence work together to narrow civic imagination and shield powerful actors from democratic accountability. Drawing on critical theory alongside historical and investigative material, the essay contends that freedom requires not only formal rights but access to an unsanitized account of the conditions that produced the present. Its central claim is that historical erasure is not secondary to domination. It is one of its primary mechanisms. [file:2]
>
> **Keywords:** hegemony, debt, media power, ideological control, historical suppression, political theology, democratic accountability

## I. The Form of Modern Obedience

The essay opens with the classic problem of voluntary servitude and uses it well. Rather than treating obedience as a mystery solved by brute force, it frames modern compliance as the effect of overlapping institutions that make domination appear normal, fragmented, and morally unremarkable. This is the right starting point because contemporary power rarely needs to announce itself as domination. It works most effectively when it is experienced as everyday life. [file:2]

That emphasis gives the paper a strong conceptual core. Citizens are not merely coerced. They are trained, indebted, informed selectively, culturally formed, and historically managed. The cumulative result is a political subject who often mistakes narrowed possibility for freedom and inherited narrative for truth. [file:2]

The essay is at its strongest when it treats these conditions not as metaphors but as social architecture. The question is not simply why people comply, but how institutions produce forms of common sense in which deeper resistance hardly appears thinkable in the first place. [file:2]

## II. Debt, Media, and Formation

The debt section is persuasive because it connects material precarity to political behavior without reducing people to economics alone. Following Graeber and related traditions, the paper presents debt as both financial mechanism and moral discipline: an arrangement that extends control into the future by binding labor, fear, and self-conception to repayment. That is a powerful lens, especially when applied to student debt, medical debt, and household financialization in modern life. [file:2]

The media section complements this well by showing how informational narrowing works structurally rather than conspiratorially. Ownership concentration, advertiser dependence, and reliance on sanctioned sources do not need to eliminate dissent entirely in order to shape public understanding. They only need to determine which claims are repeated, normalized, and granted legitimacy often enough to define reality for most citizens. [file:2]

Together, these sections show why domination in modern societies is difficult to confront directly. People are pressured not only by what they believe, but by what they owe, what they fear losing, and what they are regularly permitted to see. Material constraint and epistemic constraint reinforce one another. [file:2]

## III. Sacred Legitimacy

The essay's treatment of religion is strongest when it remains focused on political function rather than theological ridicule. Its argument is that religious systems can become civil technologies, helping sanctify hierarchy, obedience, and inherited authority by presenting historically layered traditions as timeless givens. That is a serious claim and, in the manuscript's best moments, a carefully framed one. [file:2]

The discussion of monotheism's historical development, Near Eastern influences, and later theological adaptation supports that argument by showing how traditions often treated as untouched revelation also bear the marks of contact, reform, and institutional mediation. The point is not that spiritual life is false. It is that power benefits when historically produced frameworks are shielded from historical inquiry. [file:2]

That distinction matters for the whole piece. Once the argument is kept at the level of institutional use, the essay becomes more precise and harder to dismiss. It is not attacking believers as such. It is examining how sacred authority can be politically mobilized. [file:2]

## IV. Historical Margins

The long section on marginalized historical episodes is the essay's most ambitious and most difficult component. Its governing idea is important: a society is shaped not only by what it remembers, but by what it is trained to leave unintegrated. Events can be publicly documented and still remain politically marginal if they are denied repetition, legitimacy, or narrative continuity. In that sense, suppression is often less about secrecy than about managed irrelevance. [file:2]

This is where the paper's methodological discipline matters most. The manuscript works best when it presents contested or under-discussed episodes as subjects for serious documentary scrutiny rather than as occasions for rhetorical escalation. When framed that way, the chapter's broader contribution is clear: democratic judgment becomes weaker when citizens are taught that some lines of inquiry are inherently disreputable regardless of evidence. [file:2]

The larger claim, then, is not that every hidden history has equal explanatory power. It is that the selective curation of difficult history is itself a political practice. A public denied continuity between events, institutions, and incentives is easier to manage because every revelation arrives as an isolated anomaly rather than part of a pattern. [file:2]

## V. Freedom Requires Memory

The paper's final argument is its most compelling one. Formal liberty is insufficient when citizens lack access to the historical conditions shaping their lives. A person who votes, works, consumes, and speaks inside systems they have been trained not to understand is free only in a diminished and procedural sense. Democratic self-government requires more than permission; it requires orientation. [file:2]

This is why the revised title, *Consent and Oblivion*, fits the manuscript well. The essay is ultimately about the relationship between managed acquiescence and managed forgetting. Power endures not only by securing obedience in the present, but by thinning the memory through which the present might be judged. [file:2]

The article's lasting value lies in how clearly it names that relationship. It argues that the recovery of obscured history is not a fringe pastime or a merely intellectual exercise. It is one of the conditions of serious citizenship. [file:2]
