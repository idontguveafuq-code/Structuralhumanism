# The Body Kept Sick

## How Regulatory Capture Converted Health into a Revenue Model

Kai Price | Structural Humanism Series | Revised publication draft

> **Abstract**
>
> This article argues that the modern American healthcare system is best understood not as a neutral market that occasionally fails, but as a political economy structured to extract revenue from human vulnerability. Through case studies on insulin pricing, FDA delay on trans fats, the opioid crisis, and the statutory prohibition on Medicare drug-price negotiation, the essay examines how regulatory capture transforms public-health institutions into mechanisms that protect profit while preserving the appearance of oversight. The central claim is that illness has been rendered economically valuable to the institutions nominally tasked with reducing it, and that this inversion is sustained by revolving-door governance, lobbying influence, and legal design rather than by medical necessity. **Keywords:** regulatory capture, healthcare political economy, insulin pricing, trans fats, opioid crisis, Medicare negotiation, pharmaceutical lobbying [file:115]

## I. Introduction

The manuscript opens effectively by grounding structural critique in an individual medical crisis. Maria's inability to afford insulin is not presented as tragic bad luck but as evidence of a system that monetizes dependence on life-sustaining treatment. This is a strong journalistic entry point because it gives the broader argument a human scale without sacrificing structural seriousness. [file:115]

The revised version is most persuasive when it frames healthcare as a designed revenue system rather than a malfunctioning one. On its own evidence, the problem is not that institutions failed to know what would reduce harm, but that the incentive structure rewarded delay, opacity, and cost maximization. [file:115]

## II. Capture as Structure

One of the paper's strongest contributions is its definition of regulatory capture as a structural process rather than a cinematic story of bribery. The emphasis on revolving-door appointments, industry-shaped incentives, and agencies staffed by people whose future careers may depend on corporate favor gives the argument analytical weight and makes it more suitable for serious review. [file:115]

This is where the manuscript becomes strongest as political economy. The argument is not that regulators are always corrupt in a narrow moral sense, but that the institutional design of healthcare governance aligns regulatory caution toward industry and regulatory harshness toward the public. [file:115]

## III. Slow Violence

The trans fat section is especially effective because it demonstrates what regulatory delay looks like when translated into human consequence. The paper argues that the science of harm accumulated over decades while meaningful restriction lagged, allowing preventable cardiovascular injury and death to continue under a regime that publicly described itself as protective. That is a compelling case study in what might be called bureaucratic violence by postponement. [file:115]

This section improves in publication terms when it emphasizes time as policy. Delay is not politically neutral when the harms are already well documented. The paper's larger point is that the state often appears passive only because the beneficiaries of inaction are organized and well represented. [file:115]

## IV. Addiction and Immunity

The opioid section is among the clearest and most powerful parts of the manuscript. It presents Purdue's OxyContin campaign as a case in which private marketing, institutional credibility, and weak regulatory response combined to scale addiction into a mass public-health disaster. The moral force of the section comes from the contrast between the scale of human loss and the limited form of elite accountability that followed. [file:115]

For publication, this material is strongest when framed as a study in asymmetric consequence. Patients, families, and communities absorbed the damage; executives and owners largely encountered negotiated financial exposure rather than proportionate criminal sanction. That imbalance is central to the article's argument about how the system distributes risk and protection. [file:115]

## V. Price by Law

The section on Medicare negotiation is important because it reveals that high prices are not simply market outcomes but statutory outcomes. The manuscript argues that Congress legally prohibited the country's largest buyer of pharmaceuticals from negotiating prices, even while other large public purchasers paid less for the same drugs. This makes pricing power visible as a legal and political construction. [file:115]

That point strengthens the essay considerably. Once pricing is shown to be structured by legislation influenced by industry lobbying, the rhetoric of natural market necessity becomes harder to sustain. The paper is at its best when it exposes this kind of design choice with plain institutional clarity. [file:115]

## VI. Practical Reform

The final reform section gives the manuscript needed discipline by moving from critique to targeted remedy. Repealing the Medicare negotiation prohibition, restoring ordinary bankruptcy protections for medical debt, and mandating transparent hospital billing are presented not as utopian reconstruction but as concrete measures that would materially rebalance the system. That practical emphasis improves the piece's credibility and editorial usefulness. [file:115]

This is also where the essay's moral claim becomes most legible. The article is not arguing that modern medicine itself is fraudulent; it is arguing that the financing and regulatory architecture surrounding medicine has been arranged to profit from sickness more reliably than it is arranged to reduce it. [file:115]

## VII. Editorial Positioning

This revised version is best positioned as a long-form essay in health policy, political economy, medical ethics, or accountability journalism. Its strongest editorial identity comes from combining narrative accessibility with institutional analysis, allowing it to function both as a serious review essay and as a public-facing argument about why the American healthcare system costs more, delivers less, and protects profit with unusual consistency. [file:115]

For publication, the manuscript is strongest when presented as an investigation into how law, lobbying, and regulatory structure convert bodily vulnerability into recurring revenue. That framing gives the piece coherence, urgency, and a clear place within contemporary debates over healthcare reform and democratic accountability. [file:115]
